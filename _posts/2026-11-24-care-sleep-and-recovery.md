Psychosis can be a devastating illness. It can disrupt relationships, education, work and the ordinary routines that give a day meaning. Families may find themselves trying to get help to someone who struggles to reach a service, while wondering how long they can keep everything going.

The three studies in this issue do not offer a timetable for any individual's recovery. They do inform our case for bringing care to the person, treating problems that affect quality of life now, and preparing for care that may be needed over many years. One tested a specialist early psychosis service, another treated insomnia, and the third followed people over decades.

Our editorial position is hopeful but realistic: treatment and support today may influence the future, although these papers cannot tell us how much for a particular person. We need a sustainable plan for today, with a safety net for setbacks and room for outcomes to change.

## 1. Staying connected is part of the work of a service

**Craig TKJ, Garety P, Power P, et al.** The Lambeth Early Onset (LEO) Team: randomised controlled trial of the effectiveness of specialised care for early psychosis. *BMJ*. 2004;329(7474):1067. [Original full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC526115/); [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj.38246.594873.7C).

LEO was an early randomised test of a whole specialist service. It reflects an effort in first-episode psychosis care to adapt delivery to the needs of the people the service aims to reach.

**Study and findings.** In one London borough, 144 people aged 16–40 with early non-affective psychosis were assigned to specialist care (71) or usual community care (73), with 18 months of follow-up. Specialist care combined outreach, medication, psychological therapy, family work and vocational support. Relapse/readmission data were available for 136 people.

Among those who initially recovered, 18/61 in specialist care and 29/61 in usual care relapsed. After adjustment for baseline differences, the relapse estimate was uncertain: odds ratio 0.55, 95% confidence interval 0.24–1.26. Specialist care retained advantages in service contact and number of readmissions.

**Limits.** The groups differed at baseline, the trial was small, and relapse ratings relied on records. Multiple outcomes increased the chance of apparently positive findings. The package cannot identify which component helped or separate specialist expertise from added resources.

**For families and caregivers.** Families can help clinicians understand where a service fits poorly with everyday life. With the person's agreement, explain what makes contact difficult: transport, confusing messages, anxiety about the clinic, or a schedule that is hard to manage. Ask whether appointment times, location or ways of communicating can be adapted.

A useful conversation might begin: “We want to stay connected, but this arrangement is not working. What could we change?” Bringing care within reach is a shared task; families should not have to compensate indefinitely for a service that is difficult to access. These suggested conversations were not separately tested by the trial.

**For clinicians.** Ask the person and those supporting them how delivery could improve. Agree on a named contact, a practical way to reconnect after missed visits, and what happens when the usual contact is unavailable. Family participation needs an agreed purpose, respect for confidentiality, and attention to what caregivers can reasonably sustain. The trial supports examining the service offer; it cannot establish which adaptation will help an individual.

**Current guidance, separately:** [NICE CG178, recommendations 1.3.1.1–1.3.1.3](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/cg178/chapter/recommendations) recommends accessible early intervention, assessment without delay, and a range of medical, psychological, social, educational and occupational interventions. These are UK recommendations; local eligibility and service arrangements need checking.

**Ask the team:** If contact becomes difficult, how will we reconnect, and who can help?

## 2. Address what makes daily life harder now

**Freeman D, Waite F, Startup H, et al.** Efficacy of cognitive behavioural therapy for sleep improvement in patients with persistent delusions and hallucinations (BEST): a prospective, assessor-blind, randomised controlled pilot trial. *Lancet Psychiatry*. 2015;2(11):975–983. [Original full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4641164/); [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/S2215-0366(15)00314-4).

BEST helped establish insomnia as a treatment target during ongoing psychosis. Its wider relevance is to addressing a distressing problem now, while recognising that this pilot did not establish broader recovery benefits.

**Study and findings.** Fifty UK outpatients with persistent distressing psychotic experiences and insomnia were randomised to adapted cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT) for sleep plus usual care (24) or usual care alone (26). Therapy aimed for about eight sessions over 12 weeks; assessment continued to 24 weeks. Outcome assessors were unaware of allocation.

At 12 weeks, insomnia scores favoured CBT by an adjusted 6.1 points (95% confidence interval 3.0–9.2). Nine of 22 assessed CBT participants, versus one of 25 controls, scored in the normal sleep range. Effects on delusions and hallucinations were too imprecise to establish benefit or harm.

**Limits.** This small pilot had no matched attention control and relied substantially on self-report; objective sleep data were less complete. Five serious adverse events occurred in three CBT participants, judged unrelated to treatment by the investigators. A small trial cannot establish uncommon risks.

**For families and caregivers.** Insomnia does not automatically mean adding a sleeping pill. Start the discussion with what is disturbing sleep and what the person wants to be able to do during the day. Feeling less exhausted, attending a class, or having enough energy for company may matter more to them than a sleep score.

**Current guidance, separately:** [NHLBI advice](https://www.nhlbi.nih.gov/health/insomnia/treatment) includes regular sleep habits, relaxation and daytime physical activity. These can be considered together, adapted to the person's circumstances. For persistent insomnia, [AASM guidance](https://doi.org/10.5664/jcsm.8986) recommends CBT for insomnia (CBT-I), and advises against relying on sleep hygiene alone. CBT-I can be used without a sleep medicine. [Combination-treatment guidance](https://doi.org/10.1007/s44470-025-00038-8) allows individual decisions about adding medication, but does not generally favour combination treatment over CBT-I alone. These approaches do not replace other indicated psychosis treatment. These general adult recommendations are separate from BEST's findings.

A family might offer a shared daytime walk or help make evenings calmer, if the person welcomes it. This is support, not a responsibility to deliver therapy or guarantee sleep. Exercise, relaxation and sleep habits were not tested as separate treatments in BEST.

**For clinicians.** Assess the causes of insomnia and discuss behavioural and psychological options alongside any medication decision. Connect the plan to a chosen daytime goal, and review both distress and functioning. BEST does not establish that better sleep removes voices or prevents relapse. Our editorial point is broader: care can address something that makes life difficult today while treatment for psychosis continues.

**Ask the team:** Could we begin with non-drug approaches, and how will we judge whether daily life improves?

## 3. What might life look like in 15 or 25 years?

**Harrison G, Hopper K, Craig T, et al.** Recovery from psychotic illness: a 15- and 25-year international follow-up study. *British Journal of Psychiatry*. 2001;178:506–517. [Original full text](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/the-british-journal-of-psychiatry/article/recovery-from-psychotic-illness-a-15-and-25year-international-followup-study/8C65E2E92C19E5A109B906F4944A9C05); [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1192/bjp.178.6.506).

This study describes several possible futures: sustained improvement, recurring episodes, or persistent illness with substantial support needs. It challenges both a promise of recovery and an assumption of inevitable decline.

**Study and findings.** Investigators followed historical cohorts comprising 1,633 people with psychotic disorders across 18 centres in 14 countries, at approximately 15 or 25 years. Follow-up information was available for 1,005 living participants; 229 had died and 399 were lost to follow-up. The 15- and 25-year findings came from different cohorts, not repeated assessments of everyone at both times.

Among the 502 assessed survivors with schizophrenia in cohorts assembled near illness onset and followed for about 15 years, **43% had experienced no psychotic episode during the preceding two years**, while **34% had been continuously ill** during that period. Others had episodic or intermediate courses.

In the established-illness cohorts contributing the longer-term picture, among 142 assessed survivors with schizophrenia, the corresponding figures were **41% without a psychotic episode** and **46% continuously ill** in the preceding two years. These different groups cannot tell us that an individual's condition will worsen between years 15 and 25.

About half in each schizophrenia group met the study's global recovery rating. This did not mean everyone was symptom-free, independent, or no longer needed treatment. People could work despite persisting symptoms; others needed considerable help with daily life. Most lived with family or friends, which did not establish that they could manage without that support. Some improved after years of continuous illness.

**Limits.** These are outcomes among assessed survivors in historical cohorts, not individual forecasts. Those lost to follow-up tended to have poorer early courses, making the observed picture potentially too favourable; the authors tested a conservative assumption that all missing cases had poor outcomes. Results varied across centres, and the study could not establish which treatment caused a better outcome.

**For families and caregivers.** “What will we have to face?” has several possible answers. There may be long periods of stability, episodes requiring renewed help, or enduring difficulties that call for continuing treatment and support. Improvement in symptoms may not bring the same improvement in work, relationships or everyday independence. Equally, ongoing symptoms need not exclude meaningful activities or valued roles.

Our editorial reading is to plan for changing levels of need over a long road ahead. Build routines and treatment relationships that can continue through setbacks. Discuss housing, finances, meaningful activity and help with daily tasks according to the person's priorities. Revisit possibilities after earlier disappointments; a difficult period does not settle the whole future.

Sustainability includes the people providing support. Ask who can share responsibilities, what respite is available, and what happens as caregivers age or become unavailable. A plan dependent on one exhausted person is vulnerable. We hope that protecting relationships and continuity today will support better long-term outcomes; this cohort does not prove that causal claim, and poorer outcomes do not mean a family failed.

**For clinicians.** Explain the range of outcomes while acknowledging substantial disability and premature death. Agree on what changes should prompt contact, whom to call during and outside service hours, and how care will continue if a caregiver or clinician becomes unavailable. Review treatment, physical health, support needs and the person's goals over time. These are editorial applications of the findings, not a care package tested by the cohort.

**Ask the team:** What support might we need over the years, and how can we build a sustainable plan with a safety net now?

## Bringing the three studies together

Get care to the person. Address problems that affect quality of life immediately. There may be a long road ahead, with setbacks and changing needs. Build a treatment plan and support network that can be sustained over time, while keeping hope connected to practical care.

These studies cannot promise that today's intervention will change one person's distant outcome. They can inform a reasoned approach: make treatment reachable, work on what matters now, and protect the relationships and services that may need to sustain care for years. Agree on one next step and a contingency plan, then keep reviewing both as needs change.
