## How care is organised is a clinical question

Clinical research often asks which medication or therapy should be offered. Another question runs through everyday practice: how should care be organised so that a treatment reaches the person, its effects are assessed, and the plan changes when improvement stalls?

IMPACT, TEAMcare, and CADET tested different answers. A care manager could coordinate follow-up. A team could address psychological and physical illness together. A service model developed in one health system could be adapted to another. Each trial made a way of organising care into an intervention whose effects could be measured.

These questions matter to clinicians and to the people receiving care. They also require careful interpretation. Changing organisation can change contact time, access to therapy, medication management, and specialist involvement together. Any benefit belongs first to the tested package; explaining which part produced it is a further task.

## IMPACT, 2002: Giving a care manager responsibility for follow-up

**Study design.** Multicentre, individually randomised controlled trial in US primary care.

**Methods and analysis.** Unützer and colleagues enrolled 1,801 patients aged 60 or older with major depression, dysthymia, or both, across 18 US primary care clinics. Individual randomisation was stratified by clinic and recruitment route, with assignments in sequential numbered, sealed envelopes: 906 received collaborative care and 895 usual care. A care manager supported medication management or brief problem-solving therapy, monitored progress, and worked with primary care and psychiatric supervision. Follow-up interviewers were masked.

Intention-to-treat analyses used repeated measurements at baseline and three, six, and 12 months. Mixed-effects linear and logistic models analysed continuous and binary outcomes, respectively, accounting for repeated observations within patients and adjusting for recruitment route and study organisation. Multiple imputation addressed missing data; deaths were excluded from follow-up analyses. The investigators used P<0.01 because of multiple comparisons.

**Findings.** At 12 months, approximately 45% versus 19% achieved at least a 50% reduction in SCL-20 depressive symptoms (adjusted odds ratio 3.45, 95% CI 2.71–4.38). This odds ratio should not be read as a tripling of response probability. Remission, defined as SCL-20 below 0.5, also increased: approximately 25% versus 8%. More people improved and remitted, while most intervention patients still had residual symptoms. These percentages incorporate imputation rather than simply dividing observed responders by everyone randomised. [Original article, Methods and Table 4](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jama/fullarticle/195599).

**Limitations and contribution.** Twelve-month interview completion was 83%; imputation cannot guarantee removal of bias from unobserved outcomes. Symptoms were self-reported, and shared clinicians could carry intervention practices into usual care. Acute suicide risk, severe cognitive impairment, and ongoing psychiatric treatment were among the exclusions. The trial supports a supervised care package with a designated coordinator; it cannot isolate the care manager’s effect from additional contact or the treatments delivered, or establish durability after the programme ended. [Comment](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jama/fullarticle/195599).

## TEAMcare, 2010: Joining efforts around the same person

**Study design.** Individually randomised controlled trial of integrated depression and chronic-disease care within one health plan.

**Methods and analysis.** Katon and colleagues randomised 214 patients with depression and poorly controlled diabetes, coronary heart disease, or both, across 14 clinics in one Washington State health plan: 106 to nurse-led collaborative care and 108 to enhanced usual care. Individual allocation used randomly varying block sizes. Research assistants were masked. Nurses coordinated depression and medical management for a year; usual-care physicians also received notification of depression and poor medical control.

The primary analysis jointly modelled depression, glycated haemoglobin, LDL cholesterol, and systolic pressure at 12 months using a scaled marginal model, allowing outcomes measured in different units to contribute to an overall test. This replaced the original medical-control composite during recruitment, reportedly before analysis data became available. Recruitment reached 214 rather than the planned 290; the authors argued that the revised model improved statistical power. These choices deserve scrutiny independently of the positive result.

**Findings.** The joint test favoured intervention (P<0.001). Table 2’s adjusted SCL-20 difference was −0.41 (95% CI −0.56 to −0.26), estimated across six and 12 months using generalised estimating equations to account for repeated measurements. The adjusted LDL difference was −9.1 mg/dL (−17.5 to −0.8) at 12 months. The systolic-pressure interval included zero. An overall positive test therefore does not establish a clear benefit on every component. [Original manuscript, Statistical Analysis and Table 2](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3312811/).

**Limitations and contribution.** Eighty-three percent completed all 12-month assessments. Incomplete follow-up, experienced nurses, one health plan, and unequal contact time qualify the result. Integrated care improved psychological and medical measures, suggesting possible cardiovascular benefit through risk-factor control. Fewer cardiovascular events were not demonstrated; the trial was underpowered for that question. It also cannot establish that improving depression caused better medical control. Its causal comparison concerns the coordinated package against enhanced usual care. [Discussion](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3312811/).

## CADET, 2013: A workable model with modest additional benefit

**Study design.** Pragmatic cluster-randomised superiority trial in UK primary care, with practices as the unit of allocation.

**Methods and analysis.** Richards and colleagues allocated UK primary care practices in clusters to reduce contamination between treatment groups. Allocation was centrally administered and balanced by site, deprivation, staffing, and practice size. The final 51-practice sample included 581 patients: 276 in collaborative care and 305 in usual care. Care managers delivered behavioural activation, medication support, monitoring, and liaison under specialist supervision. Researchers were masked; patients and clinicians were not.

The primary outcome was PHQ-9 depression severity at four months; 12-month severity was secondary. Under a predefined analysis plan, intention-to-treat comparisons used available outcome data with linear or logistic regression. Robust standard errors accounted for clustering within practices; covariate adjustment included allocation factors, age, and the relevant baseline score. Thus the analysis recognised that patients from one practice do not supply independent practice comparisons. Multiple-imputation sensitivity analyses produced similar results, providing reassurance within their missing-data assumptions.

**Findings.** At four months, adjusted PHQ-9 scores were 1.33 points lower (95% CI 0.35–2.31 lower; P=0.009), with data for 505 participants. The standardised effect was 0.26, below the 0.4 used for sample-size planning. At 12 months, the difference was 1.36 points (0.07–2.64; P=0.04), with data for 498. Anxiety and physical quality-of-life comparisons showed no clear benefit. This was modest statistically significant superiority for depression; the trial did not test a prespecified noninferiority margin. [Original article, Methods and Table 2](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3746956/).

**Limitations and contribution.** Cluster trials can introduce selection differences when patients are recruited after practices are allocated. Unmasked participants reported their own symptoms; expectation could influence reports, although its contribution cannot be quantified here. Supervision came from senior investigators. CADET demonstrated a deliverable adaptation with modest additional depression benefit. The remaining questions concern its magnitude, transfer to routine staffing, and whether it warrants reorganisation. [Discussion](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3746956/).

## What is being tested when a service changes?

These trials place the organisation of care alongside the choice of medication or therapy as an object of clinical investigation. In them, familiar treatments sit within new arrangements for access, monitoring, communication, and responsibility. A service can change outcomes by changing how those treatments are delivered.

There is still a scientific hypothesis: offering a specified service package will improve specified outcomes compared with the care otherwise available. Randomisation helps test that causal proposition. The conclusion is about the package. It offers less explanation of the mechanisms producing benefit or the elements necessary to reproduce it.

That distinction becomes especially important when average effects are limited. A small overall difference leaves little room to confidently attribute benefit to a particular component. More contact, closer monitoring, medication changes, and psychological treatment can all move together. Separating their contributions requires comparisons designed for that purpose; renaming the package does not resolve the uncertainty.

Service research can therefore establish evidence for reorganising care while leaving many questions about treatment mechanisms unanswered. This is a legitimate clinical purpose. The concern arises when evidence that an arrangement helped in one comparison becomes a broader claim that its organisational principle has been explained or will work wherever adopted.

## A sensible arrangement still needs a meaningful comparison

A coordinated team, a named care manager, and attention to the whole person are persuasive ideas. Their value should be judged against what patients already receive, the outcomes that matter to them, and the resources needed to sustain the change. Common sense supplies a reason to test an arrangement; it cannot specify the size or durability of its benefit.

CADET makes this tension concrete. A statistically significant, modest improvement can coexist with a reasonable question about whether a service should reorganise around it. An inconclusive secondary outcome also leaves uncertainty; it does not establish that the two approaches are equivalent. Unblinded care warrants scrutiny, while the amount of any resulting bias remains unknown.

For services today, these historical studies offer an appraisal framework rather than a verdict on current arrangements. Ask what would actually change: responsibility for follow-up, access to treatment, intensity of contact, or decisions about nonresponse. Then ask whether the observed improvement is sufficient to justify those changes locally. A reorganisation deserves the same discipline as a new treatment: a clear comparator, outcomes patients value, and conclusions proportionate to the evidence.

## Questions for a journal club

1. In IMPACT, what did the care manager change about a patient’s route through care? Which contributions can the trial distinguish from the rest of the package?
2. How would you explain benefit when response and remission increase but most patients still do not remit?
3. What makes TEAMcare’s cardiovascular implications plausible, and what evidence would be needed to demonstrate fewer cardiovascular events?
4. CADET showed a modest depression benefit. What size and type of improvement would justify the staff time and disruption of reorganisation in your service?
5. What comparison would help separate an organisational change’s effect from additional contact or changes in the treatments delivered?
