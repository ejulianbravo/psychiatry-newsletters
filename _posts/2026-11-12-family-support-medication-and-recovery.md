## Introduction

What support should a person experiencing psychosis, and the people close to them, be able to ask for? This issue approaches that question through three kinds of evidence: a family-intervention trial, a review of maintenance medication trials, and a study of coordinated early care.

These papers address different parts of care. Leff and colleagues tested structured work with families alongside medication. Leucht and colleagues brought together evidence about continuing medication after stabilization. Kane and colleagues evaluated a coordinated approach that included everyday goals.

Here, *relapse* means a return or worsening of symptoms according to a study's criteria. It does not mean that a person has failed. The findings describe groups of participants; they cannot predict an individual's future or prescribe a single route to recovery.

## Paper 1: Families deserve structured support

**Reference:** Leff J, Kuipers L, Berkowitz R, Eberlein-Vries R, Sturgeon D. (1982). *A controlled trial of social intervention in the families of schizophrenic patients.* British Journal of Psychiatry, 141(2), 121–134. [DOI: 10.1192/bjp.141.2.121](https://doi.org/10.1192/bjp.141.2.121). PMID: 7116051. The original title retains historical terminology.

### Why this paper matters

This early randomized trial helped establish family work as an intervention that could be evaluated systematically. Its practical question remains relevant: can services offer families more than routine appointments and general reassurance?

### How the study worked

The London-based team recruited people hospitalized with schizophrenia who had substantial contact with relatives rated high in *expressed emotion*: a research measure involving criticism and emotional overinvolvement. This term describes measured responses in a particular research setting; it is not a diagnosis or a moral judgment about a family.

Twenty-four families were allocated using a random-number table: twelve to routine outpatient care and twelve to additional family work. The intervention combined education in everyday language, a relatives' group, and family sessions adapted to each family's needs. Participants continued antipsychotic medication; almost all received long-acting injections.

The main follow-up was nine months after discharge. Relapse was assessed through psychiatric interviews, including recurrence of symptoms in people whose symptoms had resolved.

### What the researchers found

The original report included outcomes for **23 participants**: one of twelve assigned to family work had remained in hospital too long to complete follow-up before publication.

Among those followed, relapse occurred in **1 of 11 participants receiving family work (9%)**, compared with **6 of 12 receiving routine care (50%)**. The absolute difference was about **41 percentage points**, with an exact p-value of 0.041 reported by the authors. This was a large observed difference, but its size is uncertain in such a small sample. These are the original report's denominators, not a combined estimate incorporating the later addendum.

### What it cannot establish

The intervention team also conducted assessments and could not remain unaware of treatment allocation, creating a risk of assessment bias. Some independent checks were used, but they did not make the entire study blinded. The sample was highly selected and excluded many people admitted with schizophrenia, limiting generalization.

Because education, group support, and family sessions were delivered together, the trial cannot identify which component produced the benefit. Nor does improvement after family work establish that families caused schizophrenia. The authors' strong claims about specific mechanisms go beyond what this small, bundled intervention can securely demonstrate.

### Implications for families and clinicians

Our editorial reading is that families should be offered practical help rather than blame. The intervention was professional support alongside ongoing treatment, not an instruction for relatives to manage everything themselves. The study's historical targets for contact time should not become rules for restricting relationships today.

**Ask the team:** Is structured family support available? Can sessions address the situations we actually find difficult? How will the person receiving care help decide who participates?

## Paper 2: Relapse prevention requires an honest discussion of benefits and burdens

**Reference:** Leucht S, Tardy M, Komossa K, Heres S, Kissling W, Davis JM. (2012). *Maintenance treatment with antipsychotic drugs for schizophrenia.* Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews, Issue 5, CD008016. [DOI: 10.1002/14651858.CD008016.pub2](https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD008016.pub2). PMID: 22592725.

This is the **2012 Cochrane review supplied for this revision**, a related report of the evidence synthesis previously cited through its Lancet article. It is not the 2020 update supplied earlier. The figures below come from the 2012 review.

### Why this paper matters

The review brought together decades of randomized evidence on a difficult decision: what happens when people who have stabilized on antipsychotics continue treatment, compared with withdrawal to placebo? Its importance lies in assessing benefits and adverse effects together.

A *systematic review* searches for studies using defined methods. A *meta-analysis* combines their results statistically. This is the synthesis requested for the issue, rather than a single new treatment experiment.

### How the review worked

The authors included **65 randomized trials with 6,493 participants**, involving people with schizophrenia or related diagnoses, in inpatient and outpatient settings. Searches extended to June 2011. They extracted data independently and used a model allowing treatment effects to vary between trials.

The primary outcome was relapse at **7–12 months**. Importantly, **24 trials with 2,669 participants** contributed to that outcome: the full review's sample was not the denominator for every result.

### Main findings and trade-offs

At 7–12 months, relapse occurred in approximately **27% with medication versus 64% with placebo**. That is about **37 fewer relapses per 100 participants** in these trial groups. The reported pooled risk ratio was **0.40, with a 95% confidence interval of 0.33–0.49**. A risk ratio below one favors medication; the interval expresses statistical uncertainty around the pooled estimate. It does not describe the range of outcomes an individual should expect.

Across sixteen trials with 2,090 participants, hospitalization was also less frequent: approximately **10% versus 26%**, with pooled risk ratio 0.38 (95% confidence interval 0.27–0.55). Follow-up for hospitalization varied between studies; these are not uniform one-year risks.

Treatment burdens were real. Across trials of varying durations, at least one movement disorder was reported in about **16% with medication versus 9% with placebo** (22 trials; 3,411 participants). Weight gain and sedation were also more frequent with medication. Risks differed between drugs, and adverse effects were incompletely reported, so pooled averages cannot substitute for discussing a specific medicine and person's experience.

### Limits of the evidence

Many studies reported randomization, allocation procedures, and blinding poorly. Nonetheless, the relapse-prevention finding persisted in sensitivity analyses that excluded studies with unclear methods.

Most studies lasted no longer than a year; evidence beyond that was much thinner. The review cannot settle the best treatment duration for an individual or establish lifelong benefit. Employment, quality of life, and mortality had substantially less evidence than relapse.

Most withdrawal was abrupt. Comparisons between studies with different withdrawal procedures did not find a clear difference, but those comparisons were observational: they cannot rule out withdrawal-related effects or establish the safety of stopping quickly. The review is not a comparison of every modern, individually planned approach to dose reduction.

### Implications for families and clinicians

The authors concluded that relapse prevention must be weighed against side effects. Our editorial interpretation is to make both visible in care discussions: symptom stability matters, and so do comfort, alertness, physical health, and the person's goals. The percentages do not determine an individual's medication, dose, or treatment duration.

**Ask the team:** What benefits are we seeing? Which side effects are affecting daily life? How are these being assessed, and when will we review the plan together?

## Paper 3: Coordinated early care and everyday functioning

**Reference:** Kane JM, Robinson DG, Schooler NR, et al. (2016). *Comprehensive versus usual community care for first-episode psychosis: 2-year outcomes from the NIMH RAISE Early Treatment Program.* American Journal of Psychiatry, 173(4), 362–372. [DOI: 10.1176/appi.ajp.2015.15050632](https://doi.org/10.1176/appi.ajp.2015.15050632). PMID: 26481174. [Full article](https://www.navigateconsultants.org/wp-content/uploads/2017/05/Kane-2015-I.pdf).

This influential trial put functioning at the centre of care. Thirty-four U.S. clinics were randomized, enrolling 404 people aged 15–40 with first-episode psychosis: 223 received NAVIGATE and 181 usual care. NAVIGATE combined medication management, family education, individual therapy, and work/education support.

Over two years, quality-of-life improvement favored NAVIGATE by 5.90 scale points (standard error 2.41; standardized effect 0.31), an average difference rather than a recovery percentage. Work/school participation and symptoms also improved more; hospitalization did not differ significantly.

Baseline imbalances and the bundled intervention limit interpretation; individual components cannot be separated. Clinically, the result supports evaluating coordinated care as a whole.

**Ask the team:** Who coordinates care? Can the person's own work, education, or relationship goals guide the plan?

## Bringing the evidence into a care conversation

These papers should not be ranked by their percentages: they studied different people, comparisons, and outcomes. Their shared value is in broadening the questions we ask about support.

Current guidance is a separate source. NICE's adult psychosis guideline recommends structured family intervention, shared medication decisions, and early-intervention services that address psychological, social, occupational, educational, and medication needs. Family intervention should include at least ten planned sessions over 3–12 months. These are UK recommendations; local service pathways may differ. [NICE CG178, sections 1.3.1, 1.3.5, and 1.3.7](https://www.nice.org.uk/Guidance/CG178/chapter/recommendations?print=true).

A starting question is: **What matters most to the person this month, what support is available, and how will we know whether the plan is helping?** Families can contribute observations and questions while respecting the person's preferences and privacy.
