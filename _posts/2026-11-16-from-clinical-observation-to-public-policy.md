Important changes in psychiatry have sometimes begun with a small study and a clinically informed question. Could helping patients examine their thinking relieve depression? Could intensive support in everyday settings replace some hospital treatment? Could nurturing family care reverse the effects of early deprivation?

This issue examines three studies alongside examples of later service development and policy. Such examples show that approaches reached public systems; they do not establish how much a particular paper caused those changes. Replication, longer follow-up, advocacy, funding and delivery capacity also matter.

## 1. Cognitive therapy: putting a clinical hypothesis to an experimental test

**Rush AJ, Beck AT, Kovacs M, Hollon S.** Comparative efficacy of cognitive therapy and pharmacotherapy in the treatment of depressed outpatients. *Cognitive Therapy and Research.* 1977;1:17–37. [Original paper](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF01173502). [Accessible full-text copy](https://dradamvolungis.com/wp-content/uploads/2012/01/efficacy-of-cognitive-therapy-treatment-for-depression-rush-et-al-1977.pdf).

**The question.** Could a structured psychological treatment compete with an established antidepressant?

**The study.** The planned 12-week comparison assigned 19 depressed outpatients to cognitive therapy and 22 to imipramine. The authors described random assignment constrained by therapist availability. Both groups improved. At treatment termination, 15/19 therapy patients and 5/22 medication patients met the authors’ BDI 0–9 category for marked or complete improvement. These are not contemporary remission rates. One versus eight patients discontinued treatment.

**What needs qualification.** Independent assessors knew treatment assignment. Cognitive therapy involved more contact, and imipramine was tapered and discontinued during the final two weeks. The authors themselves questioned whether dose reduction affected the comparison. Together with the small sample, unequal dropout and developer setting, these features limit claims of superiority. The trial did not establish cognitive change as the mechanism of benefit.

**From finding to services.** England’s NHS Talking Therapies recorded **1.21 million referrals accessing services and 670,419 referrals completing a course of treatment in 2024–25**. These are programme-wide referral counts covering several therapies, including CBT. They illustrate the scale of psychological treatment services, rather than a measured effect of this single trial. [NHS annual report](https://digital.nhs.uk/data-and-information/publications/statistical/nhs-talking-therapies-for-anxiety-and-depression-annual-reports/2024-25).

## 2. Assertive community treatment: testing a different way to organize care

**Stein LI, Test MA.** Alternative to mental hospital treatment. I. Conceptual model, treatment program, and clinical evaluation. *Archives of General Psychiatry.* 1980;37(4):392–397. [Original paper](https://doi.org/10.1001/archpsyc.1980.01780170034003). PMID: 7362425.

**The question.** Could intensive treatment and practical support in everyday settings provide an alternative to hospital care?

**The study.** The investigators randomly assigned 130 adults seeking admission to a Wisconsin state hospital: 65 to Training in Community Living and 65 to hospital treatment with community aftercare. The community programme provided individually tailored support, medication and staff availability around the clock for 14 months. Assessments continued every four months to month 28.

**The findings.** Community treatment reduced hospital use during the first year. At the 12-month assessment, those with available data had spent an average of **4.7% versus 13.1% of the preceding four months in psychiatric hospitals**. Community patients also spent more time living independently. Following transfer to usual services, hospital use increased and several advantages, including those in symptoms and life satisfaction, faded. Some gains persisted, including higher competitive earnings and social-group participation.

**What needs qualification.** The study supports attention to continuing support, but withdrawal was not separately randomized against continued treatment. It cannot establish the best duration of care. Missing interviews, clinically assigned diagnoses and the single-community setting limit interpretation. The service package also combined psychosocial work with medication support; their contributions cannot be separated.

**From finding to services.** Ontario’s ACT and FACT association lists **79 provincially funded ACT teams serving close to 6,000 clients**. Its webpage does not clearly date these counts, so they indicate established adoption rather than a verified 2026 census. [Ontario ACT figures](https://ontarioassociationforactandfact.com/about-oaaf/ontario-act-fast-facts/).

## 3. Early deprivation: observing what changes when the environment changes

**Rutter M; English and Romanian Adoptees Study Team.** Developmental catch-up, and deficit, following adoption after severe global early privation. *Journal of Child Psychology and Psychiatry.* 1998;39(4):465–476. [Original paper](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0021963098002236). [Publisher record](https://doi.org/10.1111/1469-7610.00343). [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9599775/).

**The question.** How much developmental recovery followed adoption after profound early deprivation, and how was it related to the timing of the environmental change?

**The study.** Researchers assessed 111 children who entered the UK from Romania before age two and 52 UK adoptees placed before six months. Most Romanian children had experienced institutional care. At age four, McCarthy cognitive scores were available for 108 Romanian and 51 UK adoptees.

**The findings.** Mean cognitive scores were **105.9** among Romanian children entering before six months, **91.7** among those entering at six to 24 months, and **109.4** among UK adoptees. There was substantial variation within groups. Earlier placement was associated with better cognitive outcomes, and developmental recovery was considerable. Physical catch-up was less complete: even the early-arriving group remained shorter and had smaller head circumference than UK adoptees.

**What needs qualification.** Development at arrival was reconstructed from parents’ retrospective accounts, so the exact amount of cognitive catch-up is uncertain. Adoption timing was not randomized and was inseparable from time already spent in the adoptive home. Nutritional and psychological deprivation also overlapped. Six months is not a universal biological deadline, and these age-four findings do not establish an individual child’s eventual outcome.

**From finding to policy.** UNICEF’s November 2024 report described Bulgaria’s expansion of social services for children and families from **241 in 2010 to 725 in 2023**, alongside closure of large institutions. These reforms also reflect children’s rights, advocacy and political investment; they cannot be attributed to Rutter’s paper alone. [UNICEF report](https://www.unicef.org/eca/reports/focus-ending-institutionalization-children-and-keeping-families-together).

## How an early finding becomes policy

These papers tested different kinds of change: a psychological treatment, a way of organizing services, and a major improvement in a child’s rearing environment. Their designs support different conclusions. Random assignment helps compare treatments, but the details of contact, medication and follow-up still matter. A natural experiment can reveal developmental possibilities while leaving uncertainty about the contribution of each environmental change.

Their shared editorial lesson is to ask what must be sustained. Cognitive therapy required a defined intervention and trained clinicians. Community treatment required services available where people lived. Recovery after deprivation occurred in a substantially changed caregiving environment. Putting such approaches into practice involves resources, organization and time, as well as evidence.

The service and policy figures above document implementation. They do not show that a single historical study caused a reform, or that every service reproduces its results. Replication, assessment of harms, longer follow-up and evaluation in ordinary settings remain necessary when deciding what findings should change care.

## Questions for discussion

Use these papers to consider how a clinical question becomes a service or policy, and what is needed to sustain its benefits.

1. What clinical problem did each study make testable, and what did its findings change about the possibilities for care?
2. What further evidence would you want before using those findings to justify a wider service or policy?
3. What people, training, funding and ongoing support would be needed to sustain the approach in everyday practice?
4. How would you check whether wider implementation delivers benefits, avoids harm and remains useful to the people receiving care?
